## Finding and evidence boundary
The public program verified is Minnesota Management and Budget's Emerging Leaders Institute (ELI). Its current application guide describes cohort learning, expert instruction, peer exchange, interviews with leaders, reflection, and 360-degree feedback. It explicitly includes accessibility, inclusion, equity, and diverse perspectives among leadership characteristics. Eligibility requires 24 months of Minnesota state service, which need not be consecutive, and leadership support plus HR approval. These are institute requirements; they do not establish when someone can begin developing leadership capability.

Source: https://mn.gov/mmb-stat/enterprise-talent-development/leadership-development-programs/2026-eli-application-information.pdf

MMB's application webpage assigns agencies their own initial application process and permits additional agency qualifications. The statewide program description includes emerging, aspiring, and senior leadership opportunities.

## What to learn from ELI
Its useful design principle is sustained development through several forms of learning and feedback. The public description supports borrowing that organizing approach while creating original DSD learning resources. It does not supply a full curriculum to reproduce, and does not prove participant outcomes in DSD.

The distinction should be stated positively: DSD provides accessible, ongoing DEIA leadership development situated in actual divisional work. ELI remains a formal complementary opportunity. Avoid describing ELI as devoid of DEIA or suggesting this program awards equivalent credentials.

## What DSD can build upon

| Public design feature | Proposed DSD application | Evidence of a useful result |
|---|---|---|
| Peer learning | Optional Learning Lab discussions around DSD decisions | A participant revises an assumption after considering another perspective |
| Reflection | Short prompts linked to a real leadership choice | Observation, interpretation, and evidence are distinguished |
| Leader interviews | Voluntary conversations about how experienced leaders navigate an equity tradeoff | A practical decision map and questions still needing exploration |
| Feedback | Accessible, developmental feedback on a specific practice | The participant can explain what they changed and why |
| Learning over time | Return to an agreed practice after trying it | A revised work product or a clearer account of what did not help |
| Formal development opportunities | Clear information about ELI and other options alongside immediate local learning | Staff can identify both an opportunity now and a possible later formal pathway |

These are recommendations, not claims that partnerships, mentoring assignments, or leadership participation have been arranged. A formal 360 instrument is not automatically part of this build; adopting one would require an appropriate tool and clear developmental use.

## Three entrances, one DEIA foundation
1. Self-directed aspiring leaders: express an interest without waiting for nomination, connect an aspiration with a capability, and choose a supported practice opportunity.
2. Colleague- or supervisor-encouraged participants: receive an invitation, retain the choice to participate, and have an explicit conversation about support and opportunity.
3. Current leaders: select a real decision or recurring pattern, examine it through DEIA, practice a change, and seek feedback on its effect.

The existing employee-life-cycle experience is the first developed learning area shared by all three. It already connects ten stages, reflection, practice resources and continuity planning. It is not yet a complete separate learning pathway for each audience.

## Succession as an organizational practice
Start with critical functions and future capability needs. Make development opportunities visible and accessible, support people in learning the work, test knowledge transfer, and retain learning across transitions. Track whether people can actually access meaningful development and demonstrate capability. A private list of favored successors is not an adequate program design; participation is not a promise of appointment.

## Why this can serve as an example across DHS
Preserve the common structure: a DEIA question, a realistic decision, a resource, supported practice, reflection, evidence, and follow-through. Let each division supply its own work situations, relationships and priorities. DSD can demonstrate that structure before colleagues decide what to adapt in their own areas. Agency-wide adoption, schedules and commitments are not assumed.
