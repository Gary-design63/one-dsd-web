# Amplify Equity: connection, learning, and ideas into practice

Program design — September 8, 2026

Status: Research-informed design under the owner's authorized direction. This document defines the experience and operating model; it does not claim the new services are implemented, staffed, scheduled, or deployed.

## Purpose and the experience

Amplify is the voluntary community and engagement part of One DSD People, Access and Culture. It gives colleagues room to enjoy one another, explore interests, learn across teams, and influence everyday work. Equity connects these opportunities through access, belonging, voice, and opportunity. Every gathering need not explicitly discuss equity or produce a recommendation.

The intended experience is: I belong here. I can find something useful or enjoyable. My contribution can receive attention without becoming another job.

The owner's account of Amplify's earlier experience informs this design privately. Public copy will not characterize past leadership or staff efforts negatively. His current relationships and professional judgment remain central. Early 2027 is a tentative program launch expectation, not a deadline for participation or delivery.

## Research and what it changes in the design

The Surgeon General's workplace framework connects community, work-life harmony, mattering, growth, and protection from harm, with staff voice and equity at its center. For Amplify, this suggests a combination of relationships, useful learning, and opportunities to influence work. It does not establish that a particular Amplify activity will improve agency outcomes. [HHS framework](https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/workplace-well-being/index.html).

NIOSH prioritizes working conditions and organizational changes in its well-being approach. Amplify should offer enjoyable breaks while also providing a route for identifying avoidable burdens. Rest, personal coping, and cheerful activities cannot substitute for addressing harmful work conditions. Well-being can support people's capacity; it is not a prerequisite someone must satisfy to contribute to equity. [NIOSH approach](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/twh/php/hierarchy/).

The National Academies describes mentoring networks, peer relationships, and explicit mutual expectations. Its evidence is largely from STEMM education, so transferring it to DHS is a design adaptation to evaluate, not proven local effectiveness. Amplify can offer several sources of support rather than assuming one senior mentor meets every need. Participants help define what useful support means. [Mentorship forms and identity](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/25568/interactive/mentorship-defined.html); [practical mentoring tools](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/25568/interactive/tools-and-resources.html).

CIPD's employee-voice guidance emphasizes opportunities to express views and influence decisions. For this program, an idea channel must include a recipient and an honest response; a submission box alone is insufficient. This is practice guidance, not evidence of an existing leadership agreement at DHS. [Employee voice](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/voice-factsheet/).

Sources accessed September 8, 2026. The activities and operating arrangements below are program design recommendations, not activities prescribed by these sources. No research claim is made that celebration automatically reduces bias or that wellness guarantees equitable behavior.

## Three entrances, many reasons to return

### Connect and discover

A welcoming place for shared interests, appreciation, cultural discovery, and everyday connection. Offer a changing selection of experiences rather than a required calendar. Someone may simply listen, browse, or enjoy an activity.

### Learn with colleagues

A place to exchange practical knowledge, explore perspectives, and find encouragement for growth. Offer brief peer exchanges, optional mentoring conversations, and cross-team learning. The program supplies background resources and preparation.

### Ideas into practice

A supported route from an observation or possibility to consideration and a response. Staff may contribute without proposing a complete solution, speaking for a group, or volunteering to implement a change.

These are choices, not stages. There is no funnel that turns a social visit into an assignment.

## A creative activity menu

These are ready-to-adapt formats, not announced events. Suggested durations describe the size of an activity, never a deadline or expectation.

| Experience | What colleagues could do | Optional connection to program resources |
|---|---|---|
| A little joy | Share a favorite book, garden photo, craft, game, or small discovery. No explanation of its equity significance is required. | Can stand alone. |
| Minnesota through our eyes | Explore a place, artwork, or community institution through public material or an optional personal contribution. | Minnesota Communities reading collection. |
| The story behind it | Bring an object, recipe, song recommendation, or tradition someone chooses to discuss. A public example works equally well. | Intercultural practice resources; no expectation that anyone represents a community. |
| Small kindnesses | Share a concrete act that made work easier or more welcoming, with permission before identifying someone. | Workplace culture and well-being theme. |
| Borrow a good idea | Two teams share one useful practice and ask what would need to change for a different setting. | Practice tools and Operationalizing equity. |
| The question we share | Colleagues from different work areas explore a common issue such as clearer handoffs or accessible meeting participation. | Understanding DHS, accessibility resources, and ASK. |
| Listen, then linger | Play an excerpt from an approved program podcast and leave room for an informal conversation. | Existing approved podcast collection; staff playback only. |
| What I wish I had known | A colleague shares a practical lesson about navigating work, finding help, or developing a skill. | Mentoring and sponsorship resource. |
| A window into my work | A willing colleague explains a work process using a non-sensitive example, followed by questions. | Understanding DHS and Areas of work. |
| One less friction | Identify an avoidable step, unclear message, or recurring meeting difficulty and explore a possible improvement. | Process-burden, plain-language, and access tools. |
| A pause that fits | Choose a quiet pause, seated activity, accessible outdoor option, or casual conversation. | Well-being resources if wanted; no health disclosure or fitness competition. |
| Opportunity exchange | Explain how an existing development opportunity works and how interested staff can access it. | Mentoring, sponsorship, and accessible leadership resources. |

Provide seated, remote, written, and listening options where relevant. Avoid requiring spending, food contributions, travel, cameras, physical exertion, or personal disclosure. Those are practical design choices that broaden participation, not paragraphs to repeat on every staff page.

For cultural celebrations, use relevant community-authored sources where available, confirm current details before promoting an event, and distinguish learning about a tradition from participating in a sacred practice. Do not assign cultural teaching to colleagues because of their identity. Tribal sovereignty remains distinct from general cultural awareness.

## Mentorship that responds to the person

Offer a choice of a one-time question conversation, a peer learning pair, a small mentoring circle, or a continuing relationship when both people want it. A network can include different people for role knowledge, encouragement, career navigation, and access to opportunities.

Begin with what the participant wants: a skill, a clearer understanding of opportunities, a question about work, or a perspective on a decision. Ask about useful communication and access arrangements. Identity-related preferences can be volunteered; do not infer preferences from race, ethnicity, disability, age, or background.

Mentors should examine how their advice reflects their own experience. Distinguish an actual role requirement from an informal convention about speaking, appearance, networking, or self-promotion. Explore options with the participant instead of treating assimilation into a familiar style as the goal. Cultural responsiveness means inquiry and adaptation; it does not mean assuming everyone from a community wants the same thing.

Keep mentoring and sponsorship connected but distinct. Advice may help someone prepare; an introduction or transparent access to a stretch opportunity may change what becomes possible. Sponsorship cannot promise a job, promotion, or preferred selection. Opportunities remain subject to the applicable agency processes.

Co-leads can facilitate introductions to willing people. Agents can prepare discussion options and retrieve resources. No automatic matching or contact should be represented as available before participant choices, actual contacts, and the communication route are established. Human mentors provide relationships and contextual experience; AI support is clearly identified as AI.

## A supported route for staff ideas

An idea may begin in a gathering, a private conversation with the consultant, or a future program submission. Start with three simple prompts: What are you noticing? What difference would an improvement make? What would you like us to consider? A suggested solution is welcome but not required.

The Chief of Staff can help clarify a staff member's wording, gather relevant program resources, distinguish evidence from an inference, and prepare an optional recommendation. Preserve disagreement and minority perspectives; a low vote count is not a reason to dismiss an access barrier.

Before an idea is shared beyond its original setting, establish what is being shared, with whom, and how the contributor wants to be involved. That is control over a new staff contribution, not reapproval of the owner's curated program content.

The One DSD Team connects an agreed recommendation to the appropriate leadership or operational contact. A named recipient and backup, the kinds of decisions they can address, and an agreed response arrangement are necessary operating commitments. Leadership participation at every gathering is not required for this route to work. The program cannot promise an official decision or response before these arrangements exist.

Suggested statuses: Received; Clarifying together; With the appropriate team; Response available; Trying an agreed change; Closed with explanation. Acknowledgment, consideration, decision, and implementation are different events. If a response has not arrived, say so. Do not show automated reminders as a leadership response.

A staff-facing update includes the idea in approved wording, what happened, the explanation, and the next step if one exists. Share only material approved for that audience. Private submissions never automatically populate a public feed. Suggestions concerning work processes can be handled here; individual personnel cases go through the appropriate existing route.

Existing owner decisions about unresolved retention and staff-record collection remain in force. This design does not activate a new collection database or invent an indefinite retention policy.

| Role | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Staff | Choose what interests them; offer questions, ideas, or experience if they wish. No obligation to lead or resolve an issue. |
| Co-leads | Shape gatherings with colleagues, welcome participation, and connect agreed questions with support. |

| Leadership and operational contacts | Consider matters within their authority and explain decisions through arrangements agreed with them. |

The 90–95% agent-driven ambition applies to suitable preparation and coordination work, not a claim that relationships or official decisions should be simulated. Verify actual tasks and receipts rather than calling configured automation operational.

## Page design

Keep the wider program navigation and homepage hero unchanged. Give Amplify a lighter visual character with open white space, a warm photograph, restrained accent color, and clear type. Avoid a wall of instructions, a mandatory journey, or a scoreboard.

Opening draft: “Good company. Shared learning. Room for ideas.”

Supporting draft: “Connect with colleagues, explore something new, or bring an idea that could make everyday work better. Find what interests you and take part in a way that fits.”

Page order:

1. Short welcome and a prominent link to the existing Amplify community, once its access and purpose are verified.
2. Three clear entrances: Connect and discover; Learn with colleagues; Ideas into practice.
3. A small changing selection of activity ideas, clearly distinguished from confirmed gatherings.
4. A featured resource or conversation starter drawn from the published program collection.
5. What we are learning and changing: real shareable updates when they exist, never fabricated examples presented as activity.
6. Discreet links to co-lead guidance and adaptable materials.

Use the existing Gatherings, Co-leads, and Materials pages for depth. Offer mentoring as a distinct subsection with a practical guide, not an elaborate enrollment process. Only expose a submission or registration control when its full delivery, confirmation, and recording behavior works.

## How to tell whether it is worthwhile

Ask brief optional questions at useful moments: Was this worthwhile? Was there a way to participate that suited you? What would you like next? Use responses to improve the experience without assigning participation scores.

For operational follow-through, count ideas with an acknowledged recipient, responses delivered, and agreed changes actually tried. Record whether evidence is a participant report, an observed process change, or a measured result. Do not interpret attendance, enjoyment, or an AI-generated recommendation as proof of equitable service outcomes.

Check whether formats work for different schedules, roles, locations, communication needs, and access needs. Use voluntary consultation and carefully aggregated evidence; do not infer individual identities or publish identifying small-group results. Explore why people return without requiring them to return.
