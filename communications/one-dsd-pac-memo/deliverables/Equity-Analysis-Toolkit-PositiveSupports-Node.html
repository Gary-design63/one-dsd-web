Restriction versus support

The same intervention can be described as either, and the description usually depends on who is doing the describing. This is usually a step 4 (benefits, burdens) question asked directly: who decided this was support rather than restriction, and how would we know if it were the other way.

Whose behavior is treated as a problem

A behavior that draws a formal response in one setting may draw none in another, and the difference is not always about the behavior itself. This usually belongs under step 2 (data) -- disaggregated by who the behavior belonged to, and by setting.

Access to positive supports across communities

Positive support strategies require training and time to do well, and both are unevenly distributed. This usually surfaces in step 2 (data) as a provider-capacity question.

An equity analysis is the point where equity stops being something we believe and becomes something we decide — done before a decision, it changes the decision; done after, it is paperwork.

Which one do I need: an analysis, or a scan?

Answer these two questions about the work in front of you.

  1. Has this already been through a full equity analysis — and were those results built into the work?
  2. Is there genuinely not enough time to do a full analysis?

Yes to either An equity scan is the right instrument. Six questions.

No to both A full equity analysis is the right instrument. Eight steps.

A scan is shorter, not lighter: it keeps every element of the full instrument and adds engagement, evidence, accessibility and accountability criteria of its own.

The 8 steps of a full equity analysis
  1. Desired results and outcomes. What are you changing, what do you want to happen, and who could this land on hardest?
  2. Data. What do the numbers already show about who is affected? What is missing, and how could you get it?
  3. Engagement. Who is most affected, who have you actually talked to, and what did they tell you?
  4. Benefits, burdens, and mitigating unintended consequences. Who gains, who carries extra weight, and how could the plan change to reduce harm?
  5. Equity impact statement. Pull the findings together in one place.
  6. Accountability, evaluation, and communication. How will you know if it worked, who is responsible, and who hears back?
  7. Alignment. How does this fit the equity work already under way?
  8. Sustainability. Is it funded, staffed and resourced to last — including the data and the engagement?
The 6 questions of an equity scan
  1. What is the desired outcome? What change do you hope to see?
  2. Define the impacted communities or groups. Be explicit to race and intersecting identities.
  3. What can you learn from the data? Use disaggregated data — numbers broken out by group, not one total.
  4. What are the equity impacts of these actions? Who benefits, who is burdened, and how will you reduce harm?
  5. How have you used what you learned to confirm or change your design?
  6. How will you communicate results to impacted communities or groups?

Terms on the real form. Disaggregated data — split by group, not one total. Intersecting identities — a person belongs to more than one group at once. Equity impact statement — the written summary in step 5. Kept in the Department's own words on purpose.

The same questions, five kinds of work

It is the same instrument whether the object is a bill, a policy, a program change, a notice, or a class standard.

A bill

Who is impacted?

A funding formula reads as neutral. Split by county and by race and the reach becomes visible.

A policy

What can you learn from the data?

A documentation rule applies equally; whether people can meet it equally is a different question.

A program change

Who benefits, who is burdened?

Moving a service online lifts a burden for some and sets one down for others.

A public notice

How will you communicate results?

A letter that only works in written English decides who can act on it.

A class standard

What is the desired outcome?

A credential the work does not need narrows the field before hiring begins.

“We don’t have that information yet”

This is a legitimate answer — step 2 asks for it directly: what additional data would be helpful, and how could better data be obtained? Naming a gap is stronger than quietly filling the box.

Write it like this: “We do not have this broken out by race. The lead agency case system holds it. Requested; expected [date]; [name] following up.”

When one group sits inside another
A change can look fine for people with disabilities as a group, and fine for Somali Minnesotans as a group, and still fail Somali Minnesotans with disabilities.

Everyone this Division serves has a disability, so every other difference — language, distance, income, race, age — arrives on top of that one.

  • Split the data twice: race and region, language and age.
  • If numbers get too small to split, say so — that is a finding.
  • Ask who would be missed by every category just used.
